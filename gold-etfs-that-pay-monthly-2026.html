Gold ETFs That Pay Monthly Dividends in 2026: $IAUI vs $KGLD vs $IGLD

Gold doesn't pay a dividend. These three ETFs sell options on gold so they can pay you every month. Here's what each one actually paid in 2026, what it costs, how the yield is built and what can go wrong.

Quick Answer

Three gold ETFs pay monthly: $IAUI (NEOS), $KGLD (Kurv) and $IGLD (First Trust / Vest). None of them owns a dividend-paying asset. They get gold exposure and sell options on it, then pay the premium out. In 2026, $IAUI paid $5.93 a share through November (latest $0.4475), $KGLD paid $4.00 (latest $0.30) and $IGLD paid $3.89 (latest $0.1136, after a big cut in October). $IAUI is the largest at about $574 million and the cheapest at 0.79%.

People search "$IAUI dividend" on this site every week, and I get why. Gold has been one of the big stories of the last couple of years, and the idea of owning gold and getting paid every month sounds like the best of both worlds.

Here's the catch most people miss: gold itself pays nothing. Every dollar these funds hand you comes from selling options, from Treasury bill interest, or from your own capital coming back to you. So before you pick one, you need to know how each fund builds its payout. Let's walk through all three with the real numbers.

How a Gold ETF Pays a Monthly Dividend

All three funds use the same basic recipe with different ingredients:

  • Gold exposure. Instead of holding bars in a vault, they get gold price exposure through gold ETPs or options on them. $IGLD, for example, uses FLEX options that reference the SPDR Gold Trust ($GLD), and keeps the rest in short-term Treasuries and cash.
  • Selling options. They sell call options (and sometimes use puts and spreads) on that gold exposure. The buyer pays a premium up front. That premium is the raw material for your monthly check.
  • Collateral. Most of the money sits in T-bills, which earn interest while backing the option positions.

The trade-off is the same as with any covered call fund. When gold rips higher, the calls you sold cap how much of that rally you keep. When gold falls, you take most of the drop, cushioned a bit by the premium. Option premiums are bigger when gold is volatile, which is why these payouts move around.

If you've read my $BTCI dividend guide, this will sound familiar. $IAUI is the same NEOS idea pointed at gold instead of bitcoin.

$IAUI: NEOS Gold High Income ETF

$IAUI is the biggest of the three, with about $574 million in net assets on November 20, 2026, and the cheapest at 0.79% (a 0.78% management fee plus 0.01% in acquired fund fees). It launched on June 4, 2025 and trades on Cboe.

NEOS describes the strategy as a data-driven call option strategy on gold ETPs. Its holdings list on November 23 showed mostly U.S. Treasury bills, a position in the Goldman Sachs Physical Gold ETF ($AAAU) and FLEX options on $GLD, including puts as well as calls. In plain English: T-bills as the base, gold exposure layered on top, and options to generate the income.

$IAUI pays monthly and usually goes ex-dividend on a Wednesday in the second half of the month and pays two days later. The latest payout was $0.4475 a share (ex-date November 18, paid November 20, 2026), and NEOS has already declared the next one with a December 16 ex-date. The amount isn't set until it's declared.

The thing I'd flag: the payout has been sliding. It started 2026 at $0.6092 in January and was $0.4475 by November, about 27% lower. That tracks the fund's price, which has come down as gold pulled back, and the MonthlyETFs.com table flags $IAUI for price decay. For the full live numbers and the "how much do I need for $1,000 a month" math, see the $IAUI scorecard page.

$KGLD: Kurv Gold Enhanced Income ETF

$KGLD is the smallest and highest yielding of the three. It had about $140 million in net assets on November 20, 2026, charges 0.99%, and launched on July 8, 2025 on Cboe BZX.

Kurv says the fund may invest in gold and gold bullion-related ETFs, ETPs and derivatives, and sells options against that exposure. It's the least specific of the three about the exact option mix, so if you want to know precisely what you own, read the daily holdings file on Kurv's site.

The payout style is different too. $KGLD pays round numbers set in steps: $0.30 in January and February 2026, $0.45 in March and April, $0.40 in May and June, $0.35 from July through October, and back to $0.30 in November. It goes ex-dividend early in the month, usually the first Wednesday, and pays the next day. The next ex-date is December 2, 2026, with the amount not yet announced.

Kurv lists a 17.50% distribution rate. That's the headline number, but notice the step down from $0.45 to $0.30 over the year. The MonthlyETFs.com table also flags $KGLD for price decay.

$IGLD: FT Vest Gold Strategy Target Income ETF

$IGLD is the veteran. It launched on March 2, 2021, has about $477 million in net assets, and charges 0.85%. First Trust is the adviser and Vest Financial runs the options.

It doesn't hold gold directly. It uses FLEX options that reference $GLD for gold price participation and keeps the rest in short-term Treasuries and cash. On October 30, 2026, First Trust showed it writing calls on about 23.86% of NAV, with about 76.14% upside participation for the month. That's a lighter overwrite than you might expect from a "target income" fund, which leaves more room for gold's moves to come through.

Here is the part you can't miss. $IGLD paid between $0.40 and $0.55 a share every month from February through September 2026. Then the October payout dropped to $0.1175 and November was $0.1136, roughly a 70% cut. That's why First Trust's 12-month distribution rate shows 26.23% while the current distribution rate is only 7.21%. If you look up $IGLD on a site that uses trailing yield, you'll see a number the fund isn't paying anymore.

Here's how I think about it. With a gold income fund, you are still making a bet on gold. The option income changes the shape of your returns (less upside, a bit of cushion on the downside, a monthly paycheck), but it doesn't change the direction. If you don't want gold exposure, no yield makes these the right fund.

How the Payouts Are Taxed

This is where these three funds differ more than you'd think:

  • $IAUI: NEOS's notices have estimated the distributions as return of capital, and NEOS points to its 19a-1 notices for the breakdown. Final tax character is set after year end.
  • $KGLD: Kurv's most recent 19a-1 notice estimated about 82% of the distribution as return of capital. Kurv says the strategy is structured to avoid K-1 reporting and aims for 60/40 tax treatment on exchange-traded option contracts, but calls those goals, not guarantees.
  • $IGLD: First Trust says distributions may include short-term capital gains and return of capital, with the final split known after year end.

Return of capital isn't taxed when you receive it in a taxable account. It lowers your cost basis, so you pay later when you sell. Just remember that ROC can also mean the fund is giving you back your own money, which is exactly what the price decay flag is warning about. Personally, I'd hold any of these in an IRA or Roth and let the tax details be someone else's problem. This isn't tax advice, so check with a tax pro and your 1099.

My Take

I've been a dividend investor for over 10 years, and I reached financial freedom at 31 by building dividend income. The lesson I keep coming back to: the yield is the output, not the input. What you're really buying is the underlying asset and the strategy wrapped around it.

So here's how I'd sort these three:

  • $IAUI is the default pick if you want monthly income from gold. Biggest fund, lowest fee, a steadier payout pattern than the other two, and NEOS's track record with $SPYI and $QQQI. Its payout has drifted down with gold, so plan on roughly 12% to 13%, not the bigger numbers from earlier in the year.
  • $KGLD pays the highest headline rate, but it's the smallest fund, the most expensive at 0.99%, and Kurv estimates most of the payout as return of capital. Higher yield here comes with more of your own money coming back.
  • $IGLD has the longest and best track record and keeps more of gold's upside, but its October payout cut changes the income math completely. Today it's more of a gold-with-some-income fund than an income fund.

Who these fit: someone who already wants a small gold position and would like it to pay them along the way. Who they don't fit: someone who wants stable income, or who thinks a high yield makes gold safer. It doesn't. I'd keep any gold income fund to a small slice of an income portfolio, next to steadier payers. Gold Monthly Dividend ETF FAQ

Which gold ETFs pay monthly dividends?

Three U.S. gold income ETFs pay every month: the NEOS Gold High Income ETF ($IAUI), the Kurv Gold Enhanced Income ETF ($KGLD) and the FT Vest Gold Strategy Target Income ETF ($IGLD). Gold itself pays no dividend, so all three generate their monthly payouts by selling options on gold exposure, plus interest on Treasury bills.

What is the $IAUI dividend?

$IAUI's latest monthly payout was $0.4475 a share, ex-dividend November 18 and paid November 20, 2026. It paid $5.93 a share across its January to November 2026 distributions, and NEOS listed a 12.14% distribution rate as of October 31, 2026. The next ex-date is December 16, 2026.

What is the $KGLD dividend history?

In 2026, $KGLD paid $0.30 a share in January and February, $0.45 in March and April, $0.40 in May and June, $0.35 from July through October and $0.30 in November, for $4.00 a share so far. It goes ex-dividend early in the month and the next ex-date is December 2, 2026.

Why did the $IGLD dividend drop?

$IGLD paid $0.40 to $0.55 a share each month from February to September 2026, then paid $0.1175 in October and $0.1136 in November. First Trust's current distribution rate fell to 7.21% while the 12-month rate still shows 26.23%, so trailing yield figures overstate what it pays now.

Are gold income ETFs a good investment?

They can make sense for investors who already want gold exposure and would like monthly income from it, but they are not a substitute for steady income. All three were down about 5% over the year to October 2026 even with distributions counted, and $IAUI and $KGLD are flagged for price decay. This is not financial advice.
