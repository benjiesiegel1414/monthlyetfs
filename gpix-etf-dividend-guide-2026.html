$GPIX is one of those funds that doesn't get the hype of the 15% yielders, and I think that's exactly why it's worth a closer look. It's built to pay a steady monthly check from the S&P 500 without giving up the whole upside, and so far it has done that pretty well.

A lot of people land on its ticker page just wanting the dates and the numbers. So I put everything in one place: every payment, every ex-date, what drives the yield, and the parts most people skip.

What Is $GPIX?

$GPIX is the Goldman Sachs S&P 500 Premium Income ETF. It launched on October 24, 2023, and until April 30, 2025 it was called the Goldman Sachs S&P 500 Core Premium Income ETF, which is why you'll still see the old name on some sites.

It's actively managed, it holds roughly the S&P 500 (about 489 stocks), and it has grown to around $6.2 billion in assets. The total expense ratio is 0.35%, but Goldman waives part of its fee to bring the net cost to 0.29%, with that waiver in place through at least April 30, 2027 per the April 2026 summary prospectus.

The goal, in Goldman's words, is current income "while maintaining prospects for capital appreciation." That second part matters, and it's what separates $GPIX from the older covered call funds.

How $GPIX Makes an 8% Yield

The S&P 500 itself only yields around 1%. The rest of $GPIX's payout comes from selling call options.

Here's the key detail. $GPIX does not sell calls on its entire portfolio. The prospectus says it writes calls on a varying slice, expected to be between 25% and 75% of the value of its stock holdings. Goldman calls this a dynamic overwrite.

  • When it sells calls on less of the portfolio, more of the stock is free to run when the market rallies.
  • When it sells calls on more of the portfolio, it collects more premium but caps more of the upside.

Compare that to $XYLD, which sells calls on essentially 100% of the S&P 500 every month. $XYLD gets a bigger premium, but it gives up almost all the upside, and over time that has shown up as a falling share price. $GPIX tries to sit in the middle: real income, but still enough stock exposure to grow.

📌 This is the part most people miss: the overwrite level is the dial that decides the trade-off between income and growth. A covered call fund that overwrites 25% to 75% will behave very differently from one that overwrites 100%, even if they hold the exact same stocks.

What Yield Does $GPIX Actually Pay?

You'll see different yield numbers depending on where you look, and they're all "right." They just measure different things:

  • Trailing 12-month yield: about 8.2%. The last 12 payments added up to $4.58647 per share. Divide by a price near $56 and you get roughly 8.2%. That's the number I use, because it's what the fund actually paid.
  • Annualized latest payout: about 8.5%. Take the October payout of $0.39702, multiply by 12 ($4.76) and divide by the price. Goldman's own "annualized distribution rate" uses this method with NAV.
  • SEC yield: much lower. The SEC yield only counts dividends and interest from the stocks, not option premium. For a covered call fund, it badly understates what you actually receive.

Our $GPIX scorecard shows 8.20% today, which lines up with the trailing figure.

$GPIX Taxes: The Return of Capital Story

This is where $GPIX gets interesting for anyone holding it in a taxable account.

Goldman publishes a Section 19(a) notice with each payment estimating where the money came from. Its October 2026 notice estimated that, for the fiscal year through September 30, 2026, about 90.7% of $GPIX's distributions were return of capital and only about 9.3% was ordinary income.

Return of capital (ROC) is generally not taxed in the year you receive it. Instead, it lowers your cost basis. You pay tax later, when you sell, and usually at capital gains rates if you've held over a year.

⚠️ Don't confuse ROC with "destructive" ROC. On an options fund, ROC doesn't automatically mean the fund is handing back your own money. It can also reflect how option gains and losses are treated for tax purposes. The way to check: look at the share price and total return. If the price is holding up and total return is solid, the payout isn't eating the fund. And remember, these are estimates. The final split comes on your 1099-DIV in early 2027.

For comparison, $QQQI and $SPYI from NEOS get their tax edge from Section 1256 index options. I broke that down in my $SPYI dividend guide and $QQQI dividend guide.

$GPIX vs $JEPI

These two are the closest match. Similar yield, both pay monthly, both avoided price decay. The difference is the engine. $JEPI holds a lower-volatility basket of stocks and generates income through equity-linked notes, so it tends to hold up better in selloffs and lag more in big rallies. $GPIX holds the actual S&P 500 and only overwrites part of it, so it tracks the index more closely. It's also a bit cheaper. If you want something that feels more like "the S&P 500 with a paycheck," $GPIX fits that better. See the $JEPI scorecard for its live numbers.

$GPIX vs $XYLD

$XYLD pays a little more but caps nearly all the upside, and it carries a price decay flag in our data. I'd rather own the fund that can still grow. That's how I think about income in general: the yield only matters if the share price isn't quietly shrinking underneath it.

$GPIX vs $SPYI

$SPYI pays a noticeably higher yield and has a strong tax setup through Section 1256 options. $GPIX is cheaper and keeps more upside. Two different flavors of the same idea.

The Real Risks

  • Capped upside. In a ripping bull market, $GPIX will lag the S&P 500. That's the price of the income.
  • Full downside. Option premium only cushions a selloff a little. If the S&P 500 drops 20%, $GPIX is going down with it.
  • Variable payouts. The payout dipped to $0.35339 in April 2026 before climbing back. Monthly income can and will move around.
  • Active management. The overwrite level is a judgment call by the managers. Good calls help, bad calls hurt.
  • Short history. It launched in October 2023 and hasn't been tested through a long bear market yet.

My Take

$GPIX is one of the more sensible ways to turn the S&P 500 into monthly income. It's cheap, it's big enough, it has grown its payout, and it hasn't shown price decay. It's not trying to be a 15% yielder, and I see that as a feature.

Who's it for? Someone who wants a core income holding that still behaves mostly like the market, and who's fine giving up some upside for a steady monthly check. If you need the highest yield possible, look elsewhere. If you want income you don't have to babysit, it belongs on the shortlist next to $JEPI.

$GPIX Dividend FAQ

What is the $GPIX ex-dividend date?

$GPIX goes ex-dividend once a month, usually on the first business day of the month, with payment about a week later. The most recent ex-date was October 1, 2026, paid October 7, 2026. The November 2026 date had not been announced as of October 22, 2026, but the pattern points to the start of November.

What is the $GPIX dividend yield?

Over the last 12 months $GPIX paid $4.58647 per share, which is about 8.2% on a share price near $56. Annualizing the latest $0.39702 monthly payment gives $4.76 per share, or about 8.5%. The number moves every month with the payout and the share price.

Does $GPIX pay monthly dividends?

Yes. $GPIX has paid a distribution every month since its first payment in December 2023, 35 payments in total through October 2026. Goldman Sachs says the fund seeks to pay monthly at a relatively stable rate, but there is no guarantee.

How are $GPIX dividends taxed?

Goldman Sachs' latest Section 19(a) notice estimated that about 91% of $GPIX's 2026 distributions so far were return of capital, with the rest ordinary income. Return of capital is generally not taxed when received but lowers your cost basis. These are estimates only; the final breakdown comes on your Form 1099-DIV in early 2027.

Is $GPIX better than $JEPI?

They are close cousins. $GPIX holds the full S&P 500 and writes calls on 25% to 75% of the portfolio, so it keeps more upside, and it costs 0.29% net versus 0.35% for $JEPI. $JEPI holds a lower-volatility stock portfolio and uses equity-linked notes. Their yields are similar, around 8%. Which fits depends on whether you want more S&P 500 tracking or a more defensive portfolio.
