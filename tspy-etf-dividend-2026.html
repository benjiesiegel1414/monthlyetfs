$TSPY is one of those funds that shows up on every "highest yield" screen and gets almost no real explanation. People see a 14% yield on the S&P 500, a check that barely moves from month to month, and they start asking the obvious question: what's the catch?

So let's go through the actual numbers. Every payout over the last year, how the 0DTE strategy works, why TappAlpha estimated that most of the 2026 payments were return of capital, and what the total return looks like next to just owning the index.

What Is $TSPY?

$TSPY is the TappAlpha S&P 500 Growth & Daily Income ETF. It launched on August 14, 2024, trades on Nasdaq, charges 0.68% a year (all of it management fee, no acquired fund fees on top) and had about $390 million in net assets as of November 24, 2026, according to TappAlpha.

The strategy has two parts:

  • Own the S&P 500. The fund holds the index stocks directly. As of November 24 it had 507 holdings, led by Apple (8.28%), NVIDIA (6.03%), Microsoft (5.91%), Amazon (3.97%) and Alphabet's two share classes (about 4.7% combined).
  • Sell same day calls. Every trading day it writes call options that expire that same day, known as 0DTE options. The premium it collects is what funds the monthly check.

Here's the part most people miss. Because the calls expire every afternoon, the fund only caps the upside one day at a time. A traditional buy-write like $XYLD sells a month of upside in one shot. $TSPY sells a sliver every day and resets the next morning. That's the pitch: collect option income while staying closer to the index over time.

The trade-off is that on big up days, the stocks rally past the strike and the fund gives that gain away. String together a lot of strong days and you lag the index, even if the monthly check looks great.

You don't have to guess with this one. TappAlpha publishes its full calendar in advance, and the next $TSPY ex-date is Tuesday, December 1, 2026, with a record date the same day and payment on Wednesday, December 2. TappAlpha's site says to invest by November 30 to be eligible.

That 0.38% SEC yield is not a mistake. It only counts dividends and interest from the stocks, minus expenses. The S&P 500 itself only yields around 1%, and the 0.68% fee eats most of that. Everything else in the monthly check has to come from option premium, or from the fund's own capital. Which brings us to the big one.

The Return of Capital Question

Look back at the table. TappAlpha's 19a-1 notices estimated that 8 of the last 12 payments were 100% return of capital, and the other four were 81% to 92%. For the November payment, the fund page says the estimate was 100%.

Return of capital, or ROC, is not automatically bad. There are two very different kinds:

  • Tax ROC. Option funds often realize losses on the short calls in up markets while the stock gains stay unrealized. On paper, there's no taxable "income" to distribute, so the payout gets labeled ROC even though the portfolio grew. This kind is a tax deferral, and it can be a feature in a taxable account because it lowers your cost basis instead of hitting you with ordinary income now.
  • Destructive ROC. The fund is simply paying you back your own money because it didn't earn enough. That shows up as a falling share price over time.

You can't tell which one you're getting from the label alone. The only way to know is to look at total return and NAV, which is exactly what I do next. Also remember these are estimates. The final split shows up on your Form 1099-DIV after the year ends.

Personally, I'd hold a fund like this in an IRA or Roth, where the ROC versus income labels don't matter at all. In a taxable account, ROC lowers your cost basis, so you'll eventually owe capital gains when you sell. Talk to a tax pro if it's a big position.

Price Decay and Total Return

This is the section I actually care about. A 14% yield means nothing if the share price melts by 10% a year.

$TSPY is flagged for price decay on our MonthlyETFs.com data table, which means the share price has been trending down while it pays out. On November 24, the NAV was $23.45. The fund has been paying around $0.29 to $0.30 a month the whole time, so a good chunk of the yield is coming back out of the share price.

So over the last year, $TSPY returned 10.76% at NAV versus 12.07% for $VOO. You gave up about 1.3 points of total return in exchange for turning most of it into a monthly check. That's actually a smaller give-up than I expected for a 14% payer, and it's the daily reset doing its job.

The catch is the shape of the return. With $VOO, almost all of that 12% stayed in the share price and compounded. With $TSPY, most of the 10.76% was paid out to you, and the share price drifted down. If you spend the checks, your share count stays the same while each share is worth a little less. That's the price decay flag in real life. If you reinvest, the math looks a lot closer to the index. One more thing to keep in mind: this fund has only been around since August 2024, mostly in a rising market. A sharp sell-off followed by a choppy, grinding recovery is the environment where covered call funds tend to fall furthest behind. We haven't seen $TSPY go through a full bear market yet.

My Take

I've been a dividend investor for over 10 years, and I reached financial freedom at 31 by building dividend income. The lesson I keep coming back to: the yield is the headline, but total return is the story. A big check that slowly eats the share price is not the same as a big check on a growing asset.

$TSPY is better than I expected on that front. The daily reset kept it within about 1.3 points of $VOO over the last year while paying out around 14%. The payout is about as steady as it gets in this category, and the dates are published a year in advance, which I love for planning.

What keeps me cautious: the price decay flag, the heavy return of capital labels, a fund under $400 million, and a track record that hasn't seen a real bear market. I'd rather own $SPYI or $GPIX as the core and treat something like $TSPY as a smaller income booster, ideally in a retirement account where the ROC labels don't matter.

Who it fits: someone who wants a big, predictable monthly check from the S&P 500 and understands they're trading some long-term growth for it. Who it doesn't fit: someone who plans to spend every dollar of income and expects the principal to keep growing on its own. $TSPY Dividend FAQ

Does $TSPY pay a monthly dividend?

Yes. $TSPY has paid a distribution every month since October 2024. The "daily income" in its name refers to the options it sells each trading day; the income is paid out monthly, usually the day after the ex-date.

How much does $TSPY pay per month?

About $0.30 a share. The last 12 payments ranged from $0.28067 to $0.30227, and the latest was $0.29680 (ex-dividend November 3, paid November 4, 2026). That works out to roughly $126 a month on $10,000 at the November 24 price of $23.47.

When is the next $TSPY ex-dividend date?

TappAlpha's published schedule shows the next ex-dividend and record date on December 1, 2026, with payment on December 2, 2026. The amount is announced on the declaration date, November 30.

What is the $TSPY dividend yield?

TappAlpha lists a 14.49% distribution rate and a 14.48% 12 month trailing distribution yield as of November 3, 2026. The 30 day SEC yield is only 0.38% because it excludes option premium. The last 12 payouts total $3.56 a share, about 15% of the November 24 price.

Is $TSPY a good investment for income?

It depends on your goals. $TSPY pays a high, very steady monthly check and returned 10.76% at NAV over the year to October 31, 2026 versus 12.07% for $VOO. But it is flagged for price decay on our table, most 2026 payments were estimated as return of capital, and the fund is young and small. This is not financial advice.
