Two days ago I broke down $GPIX, Goldman's S&P 500 income fund. A lot of you asked for the Nasdaq-100 version, and honestly, $GPIQ is the one I find more interesting. Same playbook, but built on a more volatile index, which means bigger option premiums and a bigger monthly check.

Most people searching "$GPIQ dividend" just want the numbers: how much, when, and is it sustainable. So here's all of it in one place, plus the stuff most guides skip.

What Is $GPIQ?

$GPIQ is the Goldman Sachs Nasdaq-100 Premium Income ETF. It launched on October 24, 2023, the same day as $GPIX, so it turns three years old today.

It's actively managed and holds the stocks of the Nasdaq-100, so you're getting the big tech names: Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft and the rest. It has grown to about $6.3 billion in assets. The total expense ratio is 0.35%, but Goldman waives part of its fee to bring the net cost down to 0.29%, with that waiver in place through at least April 30, 2027 per the April 2026 summary prospectus.

The stated goal is "current income while maintaining prospects for capital appreciation." In plain English: pay me every month, but don't kill the growth. That's the whole test for a fund like this.

How $GPIQ Makes a 10% Yield

The Nasdaq-100 pays almost nothing in dividends on its own. Nearly all of $GPIQ's payout comes from selling call options.

The detail that matters: $GPIQ does not sell calls against its whole portfolio. The prospectus says the overwrite level is expected to sit between 25% and 75% of the value of its stock holdings. Goldman calls this a dynamic overwrite. It also sells those calls on things that track the benchmark, like an index ETF, rather than on the individual stocks it owns.

  • Overwrite less of the portfolio, and more of the stock is free to run when tech rallies.
  • Overwrite more of the portfolio, and the fund collects more premium but caps more of the upside.

Why is the yield higher than $GPIX? Because the Nasdaq-100 is more volatile than the S&P 500, and options on a more volatile index cost more. Selling more expensive options means more premium coming in. Same engine, more fuel.

📌 This is the part most people miss: $QYLD also sells calls on the Nasdaq-100, but on essentially 100% of the portfolio. That's why $QYLD has struggled to grow over time and why it carries a price decay flag in our data. $GPIQ's 25% to 75% range is the whole difference between "income fund that grows" and "income fund that slowly shrinks."

What Yield Does $GPIQ Actually Pay?

You'll see different yield numbers for $GPIQ depending on the site. They're all technically correct. They just measure different things:

  • Trailing 12-month yield: about 10.4%. The last 12 payments added up to $5.76565 per share. Divide by a price near $55.21 and you get roughly 10.4%. This is the one I use, because it's what the fund actually paid.
  • Annualized latest payout: about 11.1%. Take October's $0.50881, multiply by 12 ($6.11) and divide by the price. Nasdaq shows this as the "current yield."
  • SEC yield: much lower. It only counts dividends and interest from the stocks, not option premium. For a covered call fund it badly understates what lands in your account.

Our $GPIQ dividend scorecard shows about 10.1% today, right in line with the trailing figure.

$GPIQ Taxes: The Return of Capital Story

If you hold $GPIQ in a taxable account, this section matters more than the yield.

Goldman publishes a Section 19(a) notice estimating where each distribution came from. Its July 2026 notice estimated that for the fiscal year through June 30, 2026, about 96.8% of $GPIQ's distributions were return of capital and only about 3.2% was ordinary income. The prospectus itself warns that the strategy "may cause a significant portion of the distributions" to be treated that way.

Return of capital (ROC) is generally not taxed in the year you receive it. It lowers your cost basis instead, and you pay tax later when you sell, usually at long-term capital gains rates if you've held over a year.

⚠️ ROC is not automatically a red flag. On an options fund, ROC often reflects how option gains and losses are handled for tax purposes, not the fund handing you back your own money. The check I use is simple: is the share price holding up and is total return solid? For $GPIQ, so far, yes. And remember these are estimates. The final split comes on your 1099-DIV.

$QQQI gets its tax edge a different way, through Section 1256 index options. I covered that in my $QQQI dividend guide.

$GPIQ vs $JEPQ

This is the matchup most people care about. Both pay monthly, both hold the Nasdaq-100 names, and neither shows price decay. $JEPQ is the giant at about $44 billion and pays a bit more, around 11.6%. $GPIQ is cheaper at 0.29% versus 0.35%, and its partial overwrite is designed to leave more room for upside. I'd call it a coin flip on quality. If you want the bigger check, $JEPQ. If you want a bit more growth and a lower fee, $GPIQ. The $JEPQ scorecard has its live numbers.

$GPIQ vs $QQQI

$QQQI pays a much higher yield, around 14%, and has the Section 1256 tax setup. It also costs more than double. Both have held up without price decay so far. $QQQI is the "more income now" pick, $GPIQ is the "cheaper and steadier" pick.

$GPIQ vs $QYLD

This one isn't close for me. $QYLD overwrites basically everything, which has capped its growth for years, and it carries a price decay flag in our data. I'd rather own the fund that can still grow its share price and its payout. You can see the damage on the $QYLD scorecard.

The Real Risks

  • Tech concentration. You own the Nasdaq-100, which is heavy in a handful of mega-cap tech names. When tech sells off, $GPIQ goes with it.
  • Capped upside. In a ripping rally, it will trail the Nasdaq-100. That's the price of the monthly check.
  • Full downside. Option premium only softens a selloff a little. A 25% drop in the Nasdaq-100 is going to hurt.
  • Variable payouts. The payout ranged from $0.43190 to $0.51923 in 2026 alone. Don't budget your rent around one specific number.
  • Active management. The overwrite level is a judgment call by the portfolio managers. Good calls help, bad calls hurt.
  • Short history. Three years old, and it hasn't lived through a long bear market yet.

My Take

$GPIQ is one of the best-built Nasdaq-100 income funds out there right now. It's cheap, it's big, it has grown its payout about 11% year over year, and it's kept pace with the market better than almost any covered call fund I track.

Who's it for? Someone who wants monthly income from big tech without giving up most of the growth, and who can stomach tech-sized swings. If you want calmer, look at its sibling $GPIX. If you want the biggest yield possible, this isn't it. Personally, I'd rather own a 10% payer that grows than a 15% payer that shrinks. That's been the whole lesson of my 10+ years of dividend investing.

$GPIQ Dividend FAQ

How much is the $GPIQ dividend?

The latest $GPIQ dividend was $0.50881 per share, ex-dividend October 1, 2026 and paid October 7, 2026. Over the last 12 months $GPIQ paid $5.76565 per share, about 10.4% on a share price near $55. Annualizing the October payment gives $6.11 per share, or about 11.1%.

When does $GPIQ pay its dividend?

$GPIQ goes ex-dividend once a month, usually on the first business day of the month, and pays about four to seven days later. The November 2026 date had not been announced as of October 24, 2026, but the pattern points to early November.

Does $GPIQ pay monthly dividends?

Yes. $GPIQ has paid a distribution every month since its first payment in December 2023, 35 payments in total through October 2026. Goldman Sachs says the fund seeks to pay monthly at a relatively stable rate, but payouts are not guaranteed.

How are $GPIQ dividends taxed?

Goldman Sachs' Section 19(a) notice from July 2026 estimated that about 96.8% of $GPIQ's distributions for the fiscal year through June 30, 2026 were return of capital, with the rest ordinary income. Return of capital is generally not taxed when received but lowers your cost basis. These are estimates; the final split comes on Form 1099-DIV.

Is $GPIQ better than $JEPQ?

They are close cousins. $GPIQ holds the Nasdaq-100 and writes calls on 25% to 75% of the portfolio, and it costs 0.29% net versus 0.35% for $JEPQ. $JEPQ uses equity-linked notes and paid a slightly higher yield, about 11.6% versus about 10.1% in our data. $GPIQ keeps a bit more upside; $JEPQ pays a bit more income.
