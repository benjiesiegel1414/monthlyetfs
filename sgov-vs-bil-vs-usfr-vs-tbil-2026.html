$SGOV vs $BIL vs $USFR vs $TBIL: Which T-Bill ETF Is Best for Monthly Income in 2026?

Four Treasury ETFs that all pay monthly and all yield about the same. Here's what actually separates them: fees, what they hold, when they pay, and which one I'd pick for parking cash.

Quick Answer

For most people, $SGOV is the best T-bill ETF for monthly income in 2026 because it has the lowest fee (0.09%) and by far the most assets ($116.9 billion). But the gap is tiny. Over the last 12 months, $SGOV, $BIL, $USFR and $TBIL all yielded between 3.64% and 3.71%. The real differences are what they hold and when they pay: $SGOV and $BIL pay in the first week of the month, while $USFR and $TBIL pay in the last week.

Look at that yield column. 3.64%, 3.66%, 3.71%, 3.66%. That's a spread of seven hundredths of a percent. On $10,000 that's about $7 a year.

This is the part most people miss. With T-bill funds, you're not picking a winner on yield. They all own the same kind of thing, so they all earn roughly what short-term Treasury rates are paying. You're picking on fees, size, structure and timing.

What Each Fund Actually Holds

$SGOV: iShares 0-3 Month Treasury Bond ETF

$SGOV holds U.S. Treasury bills that mature in three months or less. About 24 holdings, launched May 2020, and it has grown into a monster at $116.85 billion. That's more than double $BIL. It's the cheapest of the four at 0.09%.

$BIL: SPDR Bloomberg 1-3 Month T-Bill ETF

$BIL is the original. It launched in May 2007, long before T-bill ETFs were cool. It holds bills maturing in one to three months, about 28 of them. Its fee is 0.1356%, a bit higher than $SGOV. Its share price sits around $91.71 and barely moves, because it pays out what it earns every month.

$USFR: WisdomTree Floating Rate Treasury Fund

$USFR is the different one. Instead of T-bills, it holds Treasury floating rate notes. These are two-year government notes whose interest resets every week based on the 13-week T-bill auction rate. So you still get a T-bill-like yield, but through a different instrument. About 7 holdings, launched February 2014, 0.15% fee.

$TBIL: F/m US Treasury 3 Month Bill ETF

$TBIL is the simplest idea of the bunch. It's built to hold the most recent 3-month Treasury bill and roll into the next one. Launched August 2022, $7.51 billion in assets, 0.15% fee. If you want to own "the 3-month T-bill" and nothing else, this is the purest version.

$SGOV and $BIL pay right after the month ends. The payment you get in early October is really September's interest. $USFR and $TBIL pay at the end of the month they earned it in.

So if you split your cash between, say, $SGOV and $USFR, you get paid twice a month. Once in the first week, once in the last week. Same total income, but the cash shows up more often. Do Fees Even Matter Here?

Yes. More than people think.

On a stock ETF that might return 10% a year, a 0.06% fee difference is noise. On a fund that earns about 3.6%, it's a much bigger slice. $SGOV's 0.09% takes about 2.5% of your yield. $USFR and $TBIL at 0.15% take about 4%.

Now, you'll notice $USFR still had the highest trailing yield despite the higher fee. That's because floating rate notes pay the 13-week T-bill rate plus a small spread, and the timing of the 12 payments matters too. So fees aren't everything. But when everything else is basically equal, I'd rather pay less. On big cash balances that's real money every year.

Taxes: Same Story for All Four

All four funds earn interest from U.S. Treasuries. Two things follow from that:

  • Federal: the dividends are taxed as ordinary income. They are not qualified dividends. Same rate as your paycheck.
  • State: interest from U.S. Treasuries is generally exempt from state income tax, and that usually carries through to funds that hold only Treasuries. But each state has its own rules for funds, and some, like California, have extra requirements. I went deep on this in is $SGOV state tax exempt, and the same logic applies to $BIL, $USFR and $TBIL.

If you live in a high-tax state, this is a big deal. It's one of the main reasons I'd hold cash in one of these instead of a regular high-yield savings account, which is fully taxable at the state level. Check your fund's year-end tax letter and talk to a tax pro.

The Risks Nobody Talks About

These are about as safe as it gets in the market. But "safe" isn't "zero risk."

  • Falling income: the big one. Your payout drops when rates drop. We've already seen about a 13% decline in $SGOV's monthly payment over the past year.
  • No growth: these prices barely move. $SGOV's 52-week range was $100.27 to $100.74. That's great for stability, terrible for building wealth. Inflation slowly eats it.
  • Price drop on ex-date: $BIL and $SGOV prices tick down when they pay. You're not getting free money by buying the day before.
  • Fund size: $TBIL at $7.5 billion is still big, but it's the smallest here. I don't worry about any of these closing.

Which One I'd Pick

  • "I just want the best default." $SGOV. Lowest fee, most assets, tightest trading. It's the one I'd tell a friend to use.
  • "I want the fund with the longest track record." $BIL. It's been around since 2007 and made it through 2008 and the zero-rate years.
  • "I want my cash paid at the end of the month." $USFR or $TBIL. Handy if your bills are due on the 1st.
  • "I want income twice a month." Split between $SGOV and $USFR.

$SGOV vs $BIL vs $USFR vs $TBIL FAQ

Is $SGOV better than $BIL?

For most investors, slightly. $SGOV charges 0.09% versus $BIL's 0.1356% and has more than twice the assets ($116.85 billion vs $48.66 billion as of October 30, 2026). Their trailing 12-month yields were almost identical, 3.64% for $SGOV and 3.66% for $BIL, and both pay on the same schedule early each month.

How much does $SGOV pay per month?

$SGOV's October 2026 payment was $0.30055 per share. On $100,000 that's about $298 for the month. Over the last 12 payments it averaged about $303 per month on $100,000, but the amount moves with short-term interest rates.

When does $SGOV pay dividends compared to $USFR?

$SGOV goes ex-dividend on the first business day of the month and pays a few days later, for example ex October 1 and paid October 6, 2026. $USFR goes ex in the last week of the month and pays two days later, for example ex October 27 and paid October 29, 2026.

Is $USFR safer than $SGOV?

Both hold only U.S. Treasury securities, so credit risk is about the same. $SGOV holds T-bills that mature in three months or less. $USFR holds two-year floating rate Treasury notes whose rates reset weekly, so its price also stays very stable. Neither is FDIC insured.

Are $SGOV, $BIL, $USFR and $TBIL dividends state tax exempt?

Interest from U.S. Treasuries is generally exempt from state income tax, and that usually passes through to funds that hold only Treasuries. Some states have extra rules for funds, so check each fund's year-end tax information and your state's rules. Federally, the dividends are taxed as ordinary income.
