My $SGOV dividend guide is the most visited page on this site, and my $BIL dividend breakdown is right behind it. People want to know exactly what their cash is paying them. So here's the third big one, and the one that works a little differently: $USFR.

$USFR doesn't hold T-bills. It holds floating rate Treasury notes. In practice it behaves a lot like a T-bill fund, but it pays on a different day of the month and its income resets in a slightly different way. If you've ever wondered why your $USFR check shows up at the end of the month while $SGOV pays at the start, this is for you.

What Is $USFR?

$USFR is the WisdomTree Floating Rate Treasury Fund. It launched on February 4, 2014 and tracks the Bloomberg U.S. Treasury Floating Rate Bond Index. As of November 6, 2026 it had about $21.33 billion in assets, according to WisdomTree.

Here's what's actually inside. As of November 6, the fund held just four U.S. Treasury floating rate notes (FRNs):

  • FRN maturing January 31, 2028: 29.87%
  • FRN maturing April 30, 2028: 29.87%
  • FRN maturing July 31, 2028: 29.86%
  • FRN maturing October 31, 2028: 10.41%

That's 100% U.S. Treasury, with an average of about 1.54 years to maturity. But here's the part that matters: the effective duration is just 0.02 years. Because the interest rate on these notes keeps resetting, the price barely reacts to rate changes. $USFR's NAV was $50.44 on November 6, 2026, and it closed at $50.45.

How Much Does $USFR Pay Per Month?

Over the last 12 payments, $USFR paid between $0.14489 and $0.17115 per share. The most recent one, paid October 29, 2026, was $0.17115, the highest of the past year.

Real dollars: $10,000 buys about 198 shares at $50.45. At the October rate, that's about $33.92 for the month. Over the last 12 payments, the same $10,000 would have collected about $371.

Same as with $SGOV and $BIL, there are three yield numbers floating around:

  • Trailing 12-month yield: about 3.71%. The last 12 payments ($1.87) divided by the $50.45 price. What actually got paid.
  • 30-day SEC yield: 4.01% as of November 6, 2026. The standardized number based on the last 30 days of income, after the fee.
  • Average yield to maturity: 4.10% as of November 6, before fees.

Here's what I actually look at: the SEC yield. Right now it's sitting about 0.3 percentage points above the trailing yield, which lines up with October's payment being the biggest of the year. If that holds, the next few checks should look more like October's than like the spring's. But that's a reading of the current number, not a promise.

$USFR Ex-Dividend Dates and Pay Schedule

This is the part most people miss. $USFR does not pay on the same schedule as $SGOV and $BIL. Those two go ex-dividend on the first business day of the month. $USFR goes ex in the last week of the month, usually between the 24th and the 28th, and pays about two business days later. For example:

  • October 2026: ex-date October 27, paid October 29.
  • September 2026: ex-date September 25, paid September 29.
  • August 2026: ex-date August 26, paid August 28.

Last year's November payment went ex on November 24, 2025 and paid November 26. So I'd expect the next ex-date in the last week of November 2026. Check WisdomTree's distribution schedule before you count on an exact date.

And December was normal last year. $USFR paid once in December 2025 (ex December 26, paid December 30) and then again in late January. No double December payment and no skipped January like $BIL had.

Why does the timing matter? If you hold $SGOV and $USFR together, you get one check at the start of the month and one at the end. That's a simple way to smooth out your cash flow. My monthly dividend ETF calendar shows which other funds pay early, mid and late month.

How a Floating Rate Treasury Fund Pays You

A normal bond pays a fixed coupon. A Treasury floating rate note doesn't. The U.S. Treasury issues FRNs with a two-year maturity, and their interest rate resets every week based on the 3-month T-bill auction, plus a small fixed spread set when the note is first sold. WisdomTree describes it simply: the notes are priced at a spread over 3-month T-bills and fluctuate with the most recent 3-month bill rates.

So two things move your monthly $USFR check:

  • Short-term interest rates. When 3-month T-bill rates go up, the notes' rates go up within a week, and so does your payout. When rates fall, your payout falls. Look at the history: mid-2025 payments were mostly $0.17 to $0.19. Early 2026 dropped to about $0.145 to $0.157. October 2026 bounced back to $0.171.
  • The number of days in the period. A payment covering more days of interest is bigger, even if rates didn't change. That's part of why months like February tend to come in smaller.

The upshot: $USFR's income tracks T-bill rates almost as closely as $SGOV does. You're not locking anything in. You're renting the current short-term rate, one week at a time.

Are $USFR Dividends Taxable?

Yes, at the federal level. $USFR's distributions come from interest on Treasury notes, so they're taxed as ordinary income, not at the lower qualified dividend rate. WisdomTree's own after-tax numbers show how much that bites: a 4.05% one-year NAV return became about 2.49% after taxes at the highest federal rate (as of October 31, 2026).

On the state side, interest from U.S. Treasury obligations is generally exempt from state income tax, and that usually carries through to funds that hold only Treasuries. The exact rules depend on your state, so check the fund's year-end tax info. I walked through how this works in is $SGOV state tax exempt, and the same logic applies here. This is not tax advice, so talk to a tax pro if it matters for you.

The Real Risks

  • Falling rates. The big one. Your income resets every week, so if short-term rates drop, your check drops fast. You can see it in the slide from about $0.18 in mid-2025 to about $0.15 in early 2026.
  • A higher fee than $SGOV. 0.15% versus 0.09%. Small, but it's there every year.
  • Inflation and taxes. A yield around 4% taxed as ordinary income doesn't leave much after inflation. This is a parking spot, not a growth plan.
  • Concentration in a handful of notes. Four holdings sounds scary, but they're all U.S. Treasury notes. The credit risk is the U.S. government, same as $SGOV.
  • Not a bank account. It's not FDIC insured, and the price can tick around by a few cents.

What's not really a risk: price decay. $USFR's NAV is about $50.44 and its effective duration is 0.02 years. There just isn't much room for the price to move.

$USFR Dividend FAQ

Does $USFR pay dividends monthly?

Yes. $USFR pays a dividend every month. Unlike $SGOV and $BIL, it goes ex-dividend in the last week of the month and pays about two business days later. The October 2026 dividend went ex on October 27 and was paid October 29.

How much does $USFR pay per month?

The October 2026 payment was $0.17115 per share. Over the last 12 payments $USFR paid $1.87 per share, about 3.71% on its $50.45 price. That is roughly $29 to $34 a month for every $10,000 invested.

When is $USFR's next dividend?

Based on its pattern, the next $USFR ex-dividend date should fall in the last week of November 2026, with payment about two business days later. In November 2025 it went ex on November 24 and paid November 26. Confirm the exact date on WisdomTree's distribution schedule.

What is $USFR's dividend yield?

As of November 6, 2026, $USFR's 30-day SEC yield was 4.01% and its average yield to maturity was 4.10%, according to WisdomTree. Its trailing 12-month distribution yield was about 3.71%. The yield moves with 3-month Treasury bill rates.

Is $USFR better than $SGOV?

They do a similar job in different ways. $SGOV holds Treasury bills maturing in 3 months or less and charges 0.09%. $USFR holds 2-year floating rate Treasury notes whose rates reset weekly off the 3-month T-bill and charges 0.15%. Their trailing yields are within about 0.1 percentage point. $SGOV is cheaper; $USFR pays at the end of the month instead of the beginning.
