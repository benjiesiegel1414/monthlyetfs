CLO ETFs have quietly become one of the biggest income stories of the last few years. $JAAA alone holds over $32 billion now. That's bigger than most of the famous covered call funds. And most people buying it couldn't tell you what a CLO actually is.

Let's fix that. Here's how they work, what they pay every month, and which ones I'd actually look at.

What Is a CLO, in Plain English?

CLO stands for collateralized loan obligation. Scary name, simple idea.

Banks make loans to medium and large companies, usually companies that already carry a lot of debt. These are called leveraged loans. A CLO manager buys a few hundred of those loans and bundles them into one pool.

Then the pool gets sliced into layers, called tranches:

  • AAA tranche (the top). Gets paid first. Lowest yield, most protection.
  • AA, A, BBB, BB tranches (the middle). Get paid after the layers above them. More yield, more risk.
  • Equity (the bottom). Gets whatever is left. Takes the first losses when loans default.

This is called a waterfall. Cash from the loans flows to the top first and trickles down. If some of the companies default, the bottom layers absorb the hit before the AAA layer loses a dollar.

That structure is the whole reason AAA CLOs have held up so well. Janus Henderson's own fund materials point to Moody's data showing no defaults on AAA or AA rated CLOs over the past 30 years. That's history, not a guarantee, but it explains why so much money has piled in.

📌 This is the part most people miss: the underlying loans are junk-rated. A AAA CLO is not a pile of safe loans. It's a pile of risky loans with a lot of other investors standing in front of you to take the losses. Big difference.

Why CLO ETFs Pay Monthly (and Why the Check Moves)

The loans inside a CLO are floating rate. They pay a base rate (SOFR, the short-term benchmark that tracks the Fed) plus a spread. CLO ETFs collect that interest and pass it out to shareholders every month.

Two things follow from that:

  • Almost no interest rate risk. $JAAA's effective duration was just 0.06 years as of June 30, 2026, per Janus Henderson. When rates rise, the payouts rise with them, so the share price barely cares.
  • The payout follows short-term rates. When the Fed cuts, your monthly check shrinks. When it hikes, it grows. Same deal as $SGOV, just with more yield on top.

So don't treat the current yield as locked in. It's a floating number, and that's by design.

AAA CLO ETFs: $JAAA and $PAAA

$JAAA: Janus Henderson AAA CLO ETF

The giant. $JAAA launched October 16, 2020 and has grown to about $32.6 billion across 633 holdings. It's actively managed and invests at least 80% of its portfolio in AAA-rated CLOs, and it only buys deals with a total size of at least $250 million.

The numbers that matter:

  • Expense ratio: 0.20%.
  • Latest payout: $0.20179 per share, ex-dividend September 30, 2026 and paid October 6, 2026.
  • Trailing yield: about 4.84% on a price near $50.61.
  • 30-day SEC yield: 4.77% as of June 30, 2026, per Janus Henderson.
  • Track record: 6.60% a year over three years and 4.85% a year over five years at NAV, through June 30, 2026.

The share price barely moves. That's the point. $JAAA is basically a cash alternative that pays more than cash.

$PAAA: PGIM AAA CLO ETF

$PAAA is the main challenger. It launched July 19, 2023, already holds about $15 billion, and at 0.19% it's a hair cheaper than $JAAA. Its latest payout was $0.1875 per share, ex-dividend September 30, 2026, and its trailing yield is about 4.65%.

Honestly, $JAAA vs $PAAA is close to a coin flip. Same type of bonds, nearly the same fee, nearly the same return over the last year (4.90% vs 4.93%). $JAAA has the longer track record and more liquidity. $PAAA is slightly cheaper. I wouldn't lose sleep over which one.

Higher Yield CLO ETFs: $JBBB and $CLOZ

These funds move down the waterfall. Instead of the AAA layer, they buy the BBB, BB and sometimes B tranches. More yield, but they're the layers that take hits first if loan defaults spike.

$JBBB: Janus Henderson B-BBB CLO ETF

Launched January 11, 2022, about $1.5 billion in assets, 0.47% expense ratio. The latest payout was $0.25138 per share (ex-dividend September 30, 2026), for a trailing yield around 6.33%.

$CLOZ: Eldridge BBB-B CLO ETF

Launched January 24, 2023, about $779 million in assets, 0.50% expense ratio. The latest payout was $0.15169 per share, ex-dividend October 2, 2026 and paid October 8, 2026. Trailing yield around 7.15%, the highest of the four.

Here's how I think about these. In a calm credit market, they're great. You collect 6% to 7% from floating rate paper. But in a real recession, when leveraged companies start defaulting, these are the tranches that get marked down hard. Expect them to behave more like high yield bonds than like cash.

If you want something in between, the $JPIE scorecard is worth a look. It's a broad multi-sector income fund that also pays monthly.

CLO ETFs vs $SGOV: Is the Extra Yield Worth It?

This is the real question for most people. If you're parking cash, why not just own $SGOV?

$SGOV holds 0 to 3 month Treasury bills and yields about 3.64% in our data right now. $JAAA pays roughly 1.2 points more. On $100,000, that's about $1,200 a year extra.

What you give up for that extra income:

  • Government backing. $SGOV is the U.S. Treasury. $JAAA is AAA-rated corporate credit structures. Both are low risk, but they are not the same risk.
  • State tax break. Most of $SGOV's income is exempt from state income tax. CLO income is not. I broke that down in my post on whether $SGOV is state tax exempt. In a high-tax state like California, that can close a big chunk of the gap.
  • Crash behavior. In March 2020, AAA CLO prices dipped sharply for a few weeks before recovering. T-bills didn't. If you might need the money in a panic, that matters.

My take: for true emergency money, I'd rather own Treasuries. For cash that's sitting there for a year or more and you won't touch in a crisis, a AAA CLO ETF is a reasonable way to squeeze out more income. You can see every detail of the Treasury side in my $SGOV dividend guide.

How CLO ETF Dividends Are Taxed

Short version: not great in a taxable account.

CLO ETF payouts come from loan interest, so they're generally taxed as ordinary income, not as qualified dividends. They also don't get the state tax exemption that Treasury interest gets. That's why a lot of people hold these in an IRA or 401(k) when they can.

Your fund's Form 1099-DIV has the final word every year, and a tax pro can tell you how it hits your situation.

The Real Risks

  • Credit risk. The underlying loans are to leveraged companies. A deep recession would test every tranche, especially $JBBB and $CLOZ.
  • Liquidity in a panic. CLOs trade less than Treasuries. In a selloff, prices can gap down for a while, even on AAA paper.
  • Falling rates. Floating rate cuts both ways. If the Fed keeps cutting, your monthly check keeps shrinking.
  • Active management. These funds rely on the managers picking good deals and good CLO managers.
  • Not guaranteed. A AAA rating can be downgraded, and principal is never guaranteed.

My Take

CLO ETFs are one of the more useful tools to come out of the last few years for income investors. They're not exciting, and that's the point.

If I wanted a cash-like holding that pays a bit more than T-bills, monthly, I'd look at $JAAA or $PAAA and not overthink it. If I wanted more income and could handle a rough patch in a recession, I'd consider a small slice of $JBBB or $CLOZ, knowing the total return hasn't been better than the AAA funds lately.

What I wouldn't do is treat a 7% CLO yield as "safe 7%." It's a credit bet. Know which layer of the waterfall you're standing in.

CLO ETF Monthly Dividend FAQ

Do CLO ETFs pay monthly dividends?

Yes. The major CLO ETFs, including $JAAA, $PAAA, $JBBB and $CLOZ, all pay distributions monthly. Their most recent payments went ex-dividend on September 30 or October 2, 2026 and were paid in early October 2026.

What is the best CLO ETF?

It depends on how much risk you want. $JAAA is the largest at about $32.6 billion with a 0.20% fee and a trailing yield near 4.8%. $PAAA is a close alternative at 0.19%. For more income, $JBBB (about 6.3%) and $CLOZ (about 7.2%) hold lower-rated tranches with more credit risk.

How much does $JAAA pay per month?

$JAAA's most recent payout was $0.20179 per share, ex-dividend September 30, 2026 and paid October 6, 2026. Over the last 12 months it paid about $2.45 per share, roughly 4.84% on a price near $50.61. The amount floats with short-term interest rates.

Are CLO ETF dividends qualified?

Generally no. CLO ETF distributions come from loan interest, so they are usually taxed as ordinary income and are not exempt from state income tax the way Treasury interest is. Check your Form 1099-DIV and a tax professional for your situation.

Is $JAAA safer than $SGOV?

No. $SGOV holds short-term U.S. Treasury bills, while $JAAA holds AAA-rated CLO tranches backed by corporate loans. $JAAA has held up well historically and pays more, about 4.8% versus about 3.6%, but it carries credit and liquidity risk that $SGOV does not.
