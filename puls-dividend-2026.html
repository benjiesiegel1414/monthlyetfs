Every week someone lands on my $SGOV dividend guide asking the same thing: how much is $SGOV paying, and is there anything safe-ish that pays more? I've covered every big Treasury cash fund on this site, from $BIL to $TBIL. $PULS is the next step out.

It's not a T-bill fund. It's an actively managed ultra short bond fund, and that difference is exactly why it pays more. Let's look at what you get, and what you give up.

What Is $PULS?

$PULS is the PGIM Ultra Short Bond ETF, run by PGIM, the asset management arm of Prudential Financial. It launched on April 5, 2018, trades on NYSE Arca, and had about $19.9 billion in net assets as of November 13, 2026. That makes it one of the biggest ultra short bond ETFs out there.

The goal, in PGIM's words, is total return through current income and capital appreciation, consistent with preservation of capital. In plain English: earn more than cash, without the price moving around much.

  • Actively managed. There's no index. PGIM's money markets team picks the bonds.
  • Very short. Effective duration was just 0.27 years and average maturity 1.37 years on October 31, 2026. A lot of what it owns is floating rate or matures within months.
  • Expense ratio of 0.15%, or $15 a year per $10,000. PGIM points out that's among the lowest-cost actively managed funds in the ultra short category.
  • Tight trading. PGIM reported a 0.02% median 30-day bid/ask spread and a 0.02% premium on November 13.

The price barely moves. $PULS's NAV was $49.53 on November 13, 2026, and its 52-week range was $49.45 to $49.81. That's a 36-cent range on a $50 share, which is about as calm as anything outside a T-bill fund gets.

How Much Does $PULS Pay Per Month?

Over the last 12 payments, from December 2025 through October 2026, $PULS paid between $0.1649 and $0.2262 per share. Most months land between $0.165 and $0.185. The $0.2262 outlier was the late-December 2025 payment, which tends to run bigger at year end.

Real dollars: $10,000 buys about 202 shares at $49.53. At October's $0.1834, that's about $37 for the month. Over the last 12 payments, the same $10,000 would have collected about $435.

You'll see three yield numbers on PGIM's site, and they measure different things:

  • Trailing yield: about 4.35%. The last 12 payments ($2.16) divided by the $49.53 NAV. What actually got paid.
  • 30-day SEC yield: 4.59% as of October 31, 2026. A standardized snapshot of what the portfolio is earning right now after fees.
  • Distribution yield: 4.45% as of October 31. The latest payout annualized against the price.

All three sit roughly 0.7 to 1 percentage point above where the Treasury bill funds are paying. That gap is the whole reason $PULS exists.

$PULS Ex-Dividend Dates and the Month-End Switch

Here's the part that trips people up. Through 2025, $PULS went ex-dividend on the first business day of the month. Then it changed. After the March 2, 2026 payment, it moved to the last business day of the month, starting March 31.

That's why the calendar looks a little strange. December 2025 had two payments (December 1 and December 30), there was no January 2026 ex-date, and March 2026 had two (March 2 and March 31). The total income didn't change, just the timing.

A few things jump out:

  • Almost no Treasuries. 0.2%. That matters for taxes, which I'll get to.
  • Very spread out. PGIM counts 384 issuers, and the largest, names like Blackstone CLO, BMW, Toyota and GSK, are each under 1% of the fund. No single blowup can sink it.
  • Investment grade. 98.3% is rated investment grade: 34.2% AAA, 23.9% AA, 18.5% A and 21.7% BBB, with 2.5% not rated. Nothing is junk.
  • Some CLO exposure. Several of the biggest issuers are collateralized loan obligations. If you want to understand that corner of the market, my CLO ETF monthly dividend guide breaks it down.

So when you ask "why does $PULS pay more than $SGOV," the answer is right there. You're lending to companies and buying securitized debt for a few months at a time instead of lending to the U.S. government. You get paid a little extra for that.

Are $PULS Dividends Taxable?

Yes, and this is where $PULS loses some of its edge. Its dividends are interest income, so they're taxed as ordinary income, not at the lower qualified dividend rate.

The bigger difference is at the state level. Treasury interest is generally exempt from state and local income tax, which is why so many people search whether $SGOV is state tax exempt. With only 0.2% in Treasury obligations, $PULS's income is generally fully taxable by your state.

Quick example. Say you're in a state with a 9% income tax. On $100,000, $SGOV's roughly $3,630 of Treasury income could dodge about $327 of state tax, while $PULS's roughly $4,350 would owe about $390. After state tax, the $720 gap shrinks to roughly $330. Still positive, but less than half. In a no-income-tax state like Texas or Florida, the full gap is yours. In an IRA, none of this matters. This isn't tax advice, so check PGIM's year-end tax letter and talk to a tax pro.

The Real Risks

  • Falling rates. The big one, same as every cash fund. The payout dropped about 15% from 2024 to 2025, and it will keep following short-term rates down if the Fed cuts.
  • Credit risk. $PULS owns corporate bonds, commercial paper and securitized debt. In a credit scare, spreads widen and the price can slip in a way a T-bill fund's price won't. It's investment grade and very short, so the damage should be small, but it isn't zero.
  • Active management. You're trusting PGIM's team to pick well. Their record so far has been good (Morningstar's 5-year ranking puts it in the top 12% of its category as of October 31), but there's no index to fall back on.
  • Not cash. It's not FDIC insured and it's not a money market fund. It can trade at a small premium or discount to NAV, though in 2026 that's stayed within a few hundredths of a percent.

What's not really a risk: price decay. $PULS's NAV has stayed in a tight band right around $49.50 to $49.80 all year. The income is real interest, not your own money handed back to you.

$PULS Dividend FAQ

Does $PULS pay dividends monthly?

Yes. $PULS pays a dividend every month. Since March 2026 it has gone ex-dividend on the last business day of the month and paid two to three business days later. The October 2026 dividend went ex on October 30 and was paid November 3.

How much does $PULS pay per month?

The October 2026 payment was $0.1834 per share. Over the last 12 payments $PULS paid about $2.16 per share, roughly 4.35% on its $49.53 NAV. That works out to about $33 to $38 a month for every $10,000 invested.

What is $PULS's dividend yield?

$PULS's 30-day SEC yield was 4.59% as of October 31, 2026, and its distribution yield was 4.45%, according to PGIM. Its trailing yield is about 4.35% based on the last 12 payments and the $49.53 NAV on November 13, 2026.

Is $PULS better than $SGOV?

$PULS pays more, about 4.35% trailing versus roughly 3.6% for $SGOV, but it gets that extra yield by owning corporate bonds, commercial paper and asset-backed securities instead of Treasury bills. $SGOV is cheaper at 0.09% versus 0.15%, has almost no credit risk, and its income is generally exempt from state tax. $PULS is the higher-income option with a little more risk.

Are $PULS dividends state tax exempt?

Generally no. Only about 0.2% of $PULS was in Treasury obligations as of October 31, 2026. Most of its income comes from corporate debt, commercial paper and securitized bonds, which is taxed as ordinary income at both the federal and state level. Check PGIM's year-end tax information and talk to a tax professional.
